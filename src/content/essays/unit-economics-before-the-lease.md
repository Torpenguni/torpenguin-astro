I've signed a lot of leases, and watched plenty of others sign theirs. The ones that go wrong almost always go wrong the same way: the founder fell in love with the location, the concept, or the food — and treated the numbers as a formality to confirm a decision they'd already made.

The numbers aren't a formality. They *are* the decision.

## Three numbers that decide everything

Before I sign anything, I want honest answers to three questions.

**1. What's my rent as a percentage of realistic revenue?**

Not optimistic revenue. Realistic. For most Bangkok F&B concepts, rent above ~20% of revenue is a slow bleed; above 30% it's a countdown. The trap is that brokers quote rent per square meter — a number that sounds reasonable right up until you divide by the covers you'll actually serve.

**2. How many times will I really turn each table?**

Founders model their projections against the busiest market they've seen. But dwell time is cultural and local. If your plan needs four table turns and the real behavior is closer to one and a half, your revenue-per-seat collapses by more than half — before anyone has done a single thing wrong operationally.

**3. What's my payback period at *that* throughput?**

Run the model at the throughput you'll actually get, not the one you'd get in a fantasy. If the fit-out doesn't pay back inside a window you can stomach, the concept is fragile no matter how good the food is.

## The discipline

> Strip away the branding and look at the skeleton. Does the business work at the throughput this location will actually deliver?

That's the whole game. Everything else — the menu, the design, the story — is what you build *on top of* an economic engine that already works. If the engine doesn't work, the story just helps you lose money more beautifully.

None of this is exotic. It's arithmetic. The hard part isn't the math; it's being willing to walk away from a location you've already fallen in love with when the math says no.
