The first Penguin Eat Shabu outlet worked because I was there. I knew every regular, tasted every batch of broth, fixed every problem before it became a pattern. That intimacy *was* the product.

Then we opened the second outlet, and the third, and eventually I couldn't be everywhere. The thing that made us great became the thing that didn't scale.

## The myth of the repeatable outlet

Everyone tells you to "systematize" before you scale. True, but incomplete. What nobody tells you is *which* things to systematize and which to deliberately leave loose.

> The goal of a system isn't to remove judgment. It's to remove the judgment that doesn't need a human, so your best people can spend theirs where it counts.

We over-systematized early. We wrote SOPs for things that should have stayed local, and we left the genuinely dangerous decisions — site selection, manager hiring — to gut feel. That's backwards. The high-variance, high-cost decisions are exactly the ones that deserve a rigorous process.

## Where scaling actually breaks

Three places, in my experience:

1. **Hiring outpaces culture.** You can train skills in weeks. You cannot transmit standards that fast. Around outlet five, we were hiring faster than we could acculturate, and quality wobbled.
2. **The founder becomes the bottleneck and won't admit it.** Every approval that still routes through you is a tax on growth. I held on too long.
3. **Unit economics that worked at one outlet quietly stop working at ten** — because head office, logistics, and management layers are real costs that a single shop never sees.

## What I'd do differently

Scale the boring things ruthlessly. Protect the few things that make the experience feel personal. And build the management layer *before* you think you need it — because by the time you feel the pain, you're already a year late.
